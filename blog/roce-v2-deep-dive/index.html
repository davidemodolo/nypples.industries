800 Gbps RoCE v2 on Huawei: Our Network Fabric Deep-Dive.

Engineering June 15, 2026 · 16 min read

Shard a 1.6T-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model across 4 nodes, and the all-to-all dispatch between experts becomes the bottleneck — not the matrix multiplications. Here’s how we built our 4-group topology to minimize cross-group communication.

The problem: MoE all-to-all traffic

In a standard dense transformer, tensor parallelism splits weight matrices across GPUs and the communication pattern is predictable: all-reduce after each linear layer. In an MoE model like DeepSeek V4 Pro, the expert layers introduce a wildcard. Each token is routed to a subset of experts, and those experts may live on different nodes. The resulting all-to-all communication is sparse, irregular, and extremely bandwidth-hungry.

At 800 Gbps per link, a single all-to-all dispatch for DeepSeek V4 Pro transfers approximately 3.2 GB of token data between nodes per layer. With 32 MoE layers in the model, that’s ~102 GB of cross-node traffic per inference request. If you’re serving 10 concurrent requests, you’re pushing over a terabyte of network traffic per second.

Why leaf-spine over full mesh

A full mesh topology seems appealing: connect every node to every other node, no bottlenecks. But it doesn’t scale. With 20 nodes, a full mesh requires 190 cables (n×(n-1)/2). With 800 Gbps transceivers, that’s expensive and cable-management-hostile.

Our leaf-spine design organizes the 20 nodes into 4 groups of 5. Each group connects to two spine switches. The topology:

  • Intra-group traffic: 5 nodes within a group communicate over a local 800 Gbps switch with sub-microsecond latency
  • Inter-group traffic: Cross-group communication routes through the spine layer at 800 Gbps with 1.2–1.8 μs additional latency
  • Total bisection bandwidth: 16 Tbps (4 groups × 2 spine links × 2 Tbps per spine port)

This topology maps naturally to MoE sharding. We place expert groups primarily within a single node group. The MoE gating network is aware of the topology: it prefers within-group experts and only routes cross-group when necessary for load balancing. This reduced cross-group traffic by 62% compared to topology-agnostic routing.

RoCE v2 tuning parameters

RDMA over Converged Ethernet version 2 (RoCE v2) is what makes 800 Gbps usable for AI workloads. Without proper tuning, you get congestion collapse. Our key settings:

ParameterValueRationale
DCQCNEnabled, alpha=1.0Critical for lossless operation; default alpha=0.5 was too conservative
ECN marking threshold40 KBLowered from default 200 KB — earlier congestion signal reduces tail latency
PFCEnabled, 2 queuesPriority Flow Control prevents buffer overflow on HCCL traffic
MTU4200 bytesJumbo frames for HCCL all-to-all; application traffic at 1500 MTU
Buffer size32 MB per portMatched to BDP (bandwidth-delay product) at 800 Gbps

The DCQCN alpha parameter deserves special attention. DCQCN (Data Center Quantized Congestion Notification) is Huawei’s congestion control algorithm for RoCE v2 — analogous to DCQCN implementations on Mellanox/NVIDIA hardware but tuned for Ascend’s HCCL stack. The default alpha of 0.5 throttles too aggressively under bursty MoE traffic. Bumping to 1.0 reduced our tail latency by 40% with no packet loss.

CANN NIC offload

CANN 8.0 introduced a NIC offload feature that moves HCCL collective operations from the host CPU to the NIC’s onboard processor. For all-to-all operations, this is transformative — the NIC can manage token dispatch without involving the host CPU at all.

We measured the impact:

Offload stateAll-to-all latencyCPU utilizationEffective bandwidth
Disabled84 μs22%612 Gbps
Enabled51 μs3%768 Gbps

NIC offload delivers a 39% latency reduction and 96% effective bandwidth utilization. The CPU savings (22% → 3%) are just as important — those cores are now free for KV-cache management and tokenizer operations.

HCCL bandwidth benchmarks

For the networking nerds, here are our iperf3-equivalent benchmarks using Huawei’s HCCL test tools:

  • HCCL all-reduce (1 GB): 756 Gbps effective, 94.5% of line rate
  • HCCL all-to-all (1 GB): 712 Gbps effective, 89% of line rate
  • HCCL broadcast (1 GB): 784 Gbps effective, 98% of line rate
  • Intra-group latency (4 KB message): 8.2 μs
  • Inter-group latency (4 KB message): 9.6 μs

The gap between all-reduce (94.5%) and all-to-all (89%) is entirely due to the sparse, irregular nature of MoE routing. We’re working with Huawei engineering on an HCCL schedule optimizer that should close this gap in CANN 8.1.

Worth the complexity?

Building a RoCE v2 fabric for 20 nodes is not trivial. The configuration took two full-time engineers about three weeks to tune. But the alternative — relying on standard Ethernet without RDMA — would cap effective bandwidth at roughly 200 Gbps per link due to TCP overhead. At 800 Gbps with RDMA, we can shard larger models across more nodes without hitting a communication wall.

For a 4-node GLM-5.2 deployment, the network investment pays for itself in GPU utilization: without RoCE v2, our GPU utilization would drop from 94% to roughly 62% because nodes would spend a third of their time waiting for token dispatch to complete.
