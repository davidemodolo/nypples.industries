Running GLM-5.2 on Ascend 910C: A Native Stack Retrospective.

Engineering July 12, 2026 · 14 min read

GLM-5.2’s HuggingFace model card confirms something unusual: the model was trained on Ascend 910B hardware. When we deployed it on our 910C cluster — a newer generation with ~320 TFLOPS FP16 per card — the performance surprised us. This is the story of what worked, what broke, and what we learned about running an Ascend-native model on Ascend silicon.

The native advantage

Most models we deploy — DeepSeek, Kimi, Qwen — were trained on NVIDIA hardware. The vLLM Ascend adapter does heroic work mapping their CUDA-trained weights to CANN operators, and it works remarkably well. But there’s always an impedance mismatch: certain attention patterns, fused kernels, and memory layouts were designed for A100/H100 tensor cores.

GLM-5.2 is different. It was trained end-to-end on Ascend 910B clusters using MindSpore 2.3. The model’s MoE architecture — 744B total parameters with 40B active — was explicitly designed for Ascend’s memory hierarchy. The gating network, the expert routing, even the positional encodings were profiled and optimized on Ascend hardware during training.

When we loaded the weights onto our 910C cluster, we saw 94% theoretical TFLOPS utilization on a 4-node shard. That’s roughly 6 TFLOPS/node lost to overhead — mostly kernel launch latency and inter-node all-to-all communication. For comparison, the same MoE architecture on CUDA hardware typically achieves 78-85% utilization for models that weren’t trained on that specific hardware generation.

CANN 8.0’s improved auto-parallel pass

CANN 8.0 ships with a significantly improved auto-parallel pass. The compiler now detects MoE expert parallelism patterns at graph-compile time and automatically inserts optimized HCCL (Huawei Collective Communication Library) primitives. In CANN 7.8, we had to manually specify tensor-parallel and expert-parallel sharding strategies via configuration files. With CANN 8.0, the compiler inferred our 4-node topology and produced a sharding plan that was within 2% of our hand-tuned configuration.

This matters for a simple reason: model deployment speed. With CANN 7.8, onboarding a new MoE model took 3-5 days of profiling and tuning. With CANN 8.0, we had GLM-5.2 serving production traffic within 18 hours of receiving the weights.

What broke during the first week

Not everything was smooth. Three issues stood out:

1. KV-cache alignment mismatch. GLM-5.2 uses a non-standard key-value cache layout optimized for MindSpore’s memory allocator. The vLLM Ascend adapter assumes a CUDA-style contiguous layout. The first 48 hours, we saw random precision degradation — tokens would be correct for the first 2K context positions and then drift. The fix was a 14-line patch to the adapter’s KV-cache reshape logic that respects MindSpore’s alignment requirements.

2. Expert load imbalance under burst traffic. GLM-5.2’s gating network exhibits a bias toward experts 3 through 7 under short-context queries (less than 512 tokens). This is a known artifact from the training data distribution — it’s not a bug, but it caused uneven GPU utilization during our initial load tests. We added a simple round-robin expert rebalancing layer in the adapter that evens out the load without affecting output quality.

3. MindSpore graph compiler vs. eager mode. Our initial deployment ran in eager mode for faster iteration. We expected the MindSpore graph compiler to outperform eager mode (as it typically does for Ascend hardware), but we underestimated the gap. Switching to graph-compiled execution improved throughput by 38% — a larger jump than we see on CUDA hardware, where the gap is typically 10-15%. MindSpore’s compiler is genuinely better at extracting Ascend hardware potential than the runtime JIT.

The prefill advantage (+12%) comes from Ascend’s wider memory bus — 64 GB HBM2E at 1.6 TB/s bandwidth gives it an edge on large-batch prefill operations.

What this means for the ecosystem

GLM-5.2 proves something important: Ascend-trained models running on Ascend hardware perform at parity with CUDA-trained models on NVIDIA hardware. The “native stack advantage” is real and measurable. As more models are trained on non-NVIDIA hardware — whether Ascend, AMD MI300X, or Intel Gaudi — the ecosystem will diversify in a way that benefits everyone.

For us at Nypples, the takeaway is clear: when a model’s training hardware matches its inference hardware, deployment gets dramatically simpler. We’re now actively encouraging model providers to publish their training hardware details alongside their weights. It matters more than most people realize.

We’ve contributed our KV-cache alignment patch and expert rebalancing layer back to the vLLM Ascend adapter. Both are available in vLLM adapter 0.8.3+. The MindSpore graph compilation flags are documented in our API docs under model-specific configuration.
